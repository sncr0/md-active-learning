What the campaigns measured

Four ways of choosing which simulations to run, scored against an analytic ground truth. The honest finding is more interesting than the hoped-for one: the naive method fails, a space-filling baseline is hard to beat, and the principled criterion wins on reliability before it wins on accuracy.

A concept page explains why this is built the way it is; this page reports what happened when it ran. Every number below is a measurement against the Kolafa–Nezbeda equation of state, evaluated on a dense grid over the whole domain — which is the entire reason to start with a Lennard-Jones fluid: the answer is known everywhere, so surrogate error is a quantity, not a guess.

01 The uncertainty is calibrated first

None of the comparison means anything unless the error bars are honest — the decision layer consumes them directly. So the estimator was validated before anything else: independent MD and the analytic EOS agree at T*=2.0, ρ*=0.3 to within 0.2σ in pressure and 0.6% in energy, where σ is the estimator's own autocorrelation-corrected standard error. The 0.6% energy offset is the expected finite-size + cutoff signature, not a bug. With that established, a surrogate that is "2σ wrong" is genuinely wrong, and an acquisition function that trusts σ is trusting something real.

02 Stage 1 — which acquisition learns fastest

All four strategies share the same 8-point initial design (integrated error ≈ 0.43), then diverge. Lower is better; the surface's true pressure ranges from ~0.1 to ~10, so an error of 0.01 is roughly 0.1–0.2% of scale.

    Worst at the end (0.0116) and it stops improving after ~32 sims. It keeps resampling the noisiest region because total predictive variance there is dominated by irreducible noise, which no amount of sampling shrinks. This is the failure the design set out to reproduce — and it reproduces across all three seeds.

  • latin hypercube · a real baseline

    Best at small budgets (n ≤ 24). Not a straw man: on a smooth 2-D surface a space-filling design is close to optimal, and adaptivity has little to exploit early. Any honest claim of an AL win has to clear this bar.

  • alc / imse · wins late, wins on consistency

    Starts worst (it front-loads the low-noise high-pressure corner and neglects the rest), crosses over around 31 sims, and finishes both lowest (0.0084) and with the tightest band by far (±0.0004 vs LHS's ±0.0017 — roughly 4× more repeatable). The decision-theoretic criterion pays off once it has enough points to reason globally.

  • epistemic · quietly solid

    Tracks LHS throughout and ends a hair behind ALC. Ignoring the noise term keeps it from the max-variance trap, but on this problem it has no decisive edge.

03 Where the error actually lives

The error map — surrogate minus truth over the whole plane, sample points overlaid — shows the story the single number hides. Almost all of the error is in the high-density, high-temperature corner, where pressure is largest and steepest. A uniform design spends samples evenly and under-resolves it; ALC concentrates there and shaves it down.

A floor everyone hits: the 864-particle, 2.5σ-cutoff simulations differ from the full-potential EOS by a small, fixed amount (~0.008). Every strategy piles up against it, so the contest is really how fast you reach the floor — not how far below it you can go.

04 The honest reading

The dramatic "active learning reaches equal error in a third of the simulations" did not happen at this budget — and saying so is the point of having a ground truth. Three reasons, all diagnosable: the surface is smooth and low-dimensional (space-filling is hard to beat); a finite-size floor caps everyone; and the metric is dominated by one corner that all strategies eventually resolve.

What the data does establish cleanly is worth more than a hyped number: the naive acquisition fails in a specific, predicted way, and the principled one is the most accurate and — the practically decisive property — the most reliable. On a real campaign you get one run, not three; a method whose error band is 4× tighter is the one you trust.

05 Stage 2 — spending a budget, not counting runs

Stage 2 lets the policy choose how long to run each simulation, under a fixed wall-clock budget. Longer runs cost more but return lower noise (σ² = K(x)/ℓ). Maximizing variance-reduction-per-cost has a closed form for the optimal production length,

ℓ*(x) = √( K(x) · c₀ / ( epistemic_var(x) · c₁ ) ),

— run longer where the noise K is large, shorter where the function is already uncertain (a cheap noisy sample there already tells you most of what a precise one would).

Against a fixed-5000-step baseline at equal budget, the cost-aware policy ran 56 shorter simulations (mean length ~3,300, adaptively 1,500–7,900) versus 48 at 5,000, and reached slightly lower error (0.0178 vs 0.0188) for slightly less spend. Spatially it allocated ~2× more production to the high-density corner (mean 3,950 steps) than to the quiet low-density majority (2,100 steps). The answer to the open question — many cheap samples, or few precise ones? — is both, placed deliberately: breadth across the easy region, precision banked where the surface is hard.

06 What would move the needle

The regimes where adaptivity should dominate are exactly the ones this smooth 2-D benchmark lacks, and they are the roadmap: higher input dimension (space-filling degrades fast); a localized target such as hunting the coexistence-dome boundary rather than fitting a smooth field; and simply a larger budget with longer runs and more seeds, to separate the curves before the finite-size floor. Cost-aware AL (Stage 2) is the first step into that territory — and the length-allocation map is the first sign the policy is reasoning about precision the way a person allocating compute would.
