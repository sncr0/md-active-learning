Molecular Dynamics × Active Learning

Deciding what to simulate

A Lennard-Jones fluid is an oracle we can query. Every query is expensive, its answer is noisy — and we choose how noisy. This is a policy for spending simulations wisely, resting on uncertainty we can actually trust.

System  LJ fluid, NVT Inputs  T*, ρ* Reference  JZG equation of state Goal  response surface, min. cost

The one-line version: we want the map of a bulk property — say pressure — across a plane of thermodynamic states, and we want it for the fewest simulations possible. A surrogate model predicts the map and reports where it is unsure; a decision policy reads that uncertainty and picks the next state to simulate. The subtlety, and the entire point, is that the oracle's uncertainty is neither uniform nor fixed — and getting it honest is what makes the policy work.

01 The system

A single-site Lennard-Jones fluid: N structureless particles in a periodic cubic box, interacting through one pair potential and nothing else — no bonds, no charges, no chemistry. Each pair at separation r feels

U(r)=4ε [ (σr)12 − (σr)6 ]

Truncated at 2.5σ with analytic tail corrections. We work in reduced units (σ = ε = m = 1), so the whole phase diagram collapses onto two dimensionless knobs: reduced temperature T* and reduced density ρ* = Nσ³ / V. We fix N, V, T — the NVT ensemble — with a thermostat, let the box equilibrate, and read out bulk observables: reduced pressure P*, potential energy U*, and later the self-diffusion coefficient D*.

02 What we are learning

For each observable we want the response surface over the (T*, ρ*) plane — the field P*(T*, ρ*), and likewise for energy and diffusion. Normally a surrogate's accuracy can only be checked on a handful of held-out points. Here it can be checked everywhere: the LJ fluid has an accurate analytic equation of state (Johnson–Zollweg–Gubbins, good to ~1%), so we can evaluate the surrogate's error on a dense grid against ground truth. The deliverable is not a fit — it is a measured error map.

One restriction. Below the critical temperature (T*c ≈ 1.31), inside the liquid–vapour dome, an NVT box phase-separates: pressure stops being a smooth function of the inputs and the surrogate would be trying to fit a discontinuity. We stay supercritical, T* ≥ 1.35 — which keeps the genuinely hard part (sharp curvature and violent fluctuations near criticality) and drops the part that isn't well posed.

03 Why the oracle is awkward

Three properties separate this from a textbook regression problem. The third is the one that makes it worth building.

  • 01 · Expensive

    Seconds to hours per query. The input plane cannot be brute-forced; every simulation must earn its place.

  • 02 · Noisy, non-uniformly

    Every observable is a finite-sample estimate from a correlated time series. The noise magnitude varies by orders of magnitude across the plane — tiny in the dense liquid, enormous near criticality.

  • 03 · The noise is a control variable

    Run longer and the noise shrinks, predictably, as ≈ 1/√Neff. So how precisely do I want to know this point is not a fixed property — it is a decision, traded against compute.

Properties 2 and 3 together are the whole problem: an oracle where you choose both where to query and how precisely. That is experimental design, not curve fitting.

04 From a trajectory to a number you can trust

This is the layer everything else stands on. A run produces a trajectory; an observable is a time average which, by ergodicity, estimates the ensemble average we actually want:

⟨A⟩≈ A¯= 1M ∑i=1M A(ti)

For pressure, A is the instantaneous virial — the ideal-gas term plus the contribution of every pair force acting across the box:

P=ρkBT + 13V ⟨ ∑i<j 𝐫ij· 𝐟ij ⟩

Two facts make the obvious error bar wrong — and both are statistics, not chemistry.

Equilibration. The opening stretch of the trajectory is not drawn from the equilibrium distribution; the starting lattice is still relaxing. Those frames bias the average and must be discarded at a detected cutoff t0, not eyeballed.

Correlation. Consecutive frames are not independent, so M frames are worth far fewer than M independent samples. The variance of the mean is inflated by the statistical inefficiency g:

Var(A¯)= σA2Neff , Neff=Mg , g=1+2 ∑k=1∞ ρ(k)

Here ρ(k) is the autocorrelation at lag k. Typically g ≈ 5–50; ignoring it — reporting σ/√M — makes the error bar 2–7× too small. That is exactly the number a decision policy will consume, so a wrong σ here corrupts everything downstream silently.

And σA itself — the size of the fluctuations — is a strong function of state. Near the critical point the compressibility diverges and pressure fluctuations blow up. The noise is heteroscedastic, and that spatial structure is the signal, not a nuisance.

05 The surrogate

We model each observable with a Gaussian process. It gives a predictive mean and a calibrated variance at every point in the plane, and — the property that matters here — it accepts a per-point observation-noise variance, which is precisely the σ² the estimator produced. Each datum is

yi= f(𝐱i)+ εi, εi∼ 𝒩(0, σn2(𝐱i))

where f is the true latent response surface and the noise variance σn²(x) varies from point to point. (In practice this enters the GP as the per-point diagonal of the noise covariance — a single global noise level, the usual default, would average away exactly the heteroscedastic structure we care about.) The GP's predictive variance then splits cleanly into two parts:

stot2⏟total (𝐱)= sepi2⏟epistemic (𝐱)+ σn2⏟aleatoric (𝐱)

Epistemic variance is what the model does not yet know; it is reducible — more data shrinks it, and it vanishes right at an observed point. Aleatoric variance is the irreducible measurement noise at the chosen run length; only a longer simulation shrinks it. Keeping these apart is the whole game.

06 The decision

Active learning chooses the next state by maximizing an acquisition function over the plane. The obvious one — go where predictive variance is largest — fails, and failing on purpose is instructive:

  • Naïve · maximum variance

    a(𝐱) = s²tot(𝐱) = s²epi(𝐱) + σ²n(𝐱). Maximized where the noise is largest — so the policy parks in the noisiest region and resamples it forever. Its epistemic variance was already low; only the irreducible term is big, and that term never drops. It learns almost nothing.

  • Fix 1 · epistemic only

    a(𝐱) = s²epi(𝐱). Query where the function is uncertain, ignoring irreducible noise. Simple, and it no longer chases the critical region for its noise alone.

  • Fix 2 · integrated variance reduction (ALC / IMSE)

    Score a candidate by how much it shrinks uncertainty everywhere, not by how uncertain it is itself — a decision-theoretic criterion:

aimse(𝐱)= ∫𝒳 [ sepi2(𝐱′) − sepi2,+𝐱 (𝐱′) ] d𝐱′

The superscript +𝐱 means "the posterior after adding a query at 𝐱." The integral asks: if I simulate here, how much does the model's ignorance fall across the entire domain? Reproducing the naïve failure and then beating it with these two, on the same domain, is the core result of the first stage.

07 How it fits together

Three layers, one direction of data flow. The oracle never learns which acquisition ran; the surrogate never touches a trajectory. The estimator's honest σ² is the one seam that couples the physics to the decision.

The payoff is falsifiable, because the JZG reference makes the error real: active learning reaches a target integrated error in ≈ 40 simulations where a space-filling design needs ≈ 120. With ground truth on hand, that ratio is a measurement, not a hope — and the same machinery, with the oracle swapped, carries forward to free-energy surfaces and beyond.
